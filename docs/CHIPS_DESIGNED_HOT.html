Chips Designed Hot

Every processor built so far treats heat as a tax. Localized photonic cooling turns it into a design variable — and once it is one, the cheapest chip and the fastest chip are both hotter than anything shipping today.

The thesis

Cooling stops being overhead and becomes a lever

A conventional cooler has one setting: more. More airflow, more flow rate, colder coolant. The expensive part is not moving the heat — it is holding the hotspot under its limit while doing so. A fan carrying a fixed load gets dramatically cheaper if you let the air run hotter, and the only reason a designer cannot is that hotter air raises the ambient the die sees and eats the margin the hotspot needs.

That is the trade the array breaks. It does not carry heat more cheaply than a fan — measured against this project’s calibrated fan it does not come close. It buys back margin, exactly where margin is spent, which lets the bulk air stream run hot and cheap. The array and the fan are not competing for the same job.

The lever

One array, two currencies

Hold the junction at its limit and let the array take flux fraction s. The package then only has to move what is left, so its required resistance relaxes by 1/(1−s). That relaxation can be spent on clock, or on the air stream. The first is a die‑thermal result; the second is now measured through the solver rather than argued.

At today’s extractor, turning the fan down does not save power. The array has to lift more heat and costs more per watt than the fan saves, so the optimum sits at the baseline airflow. An earlier version of this page claimed a 22–35 % system saving from displacing a fan whose power falls as the fifth power of resistance — that used the textbook affinity law, and this project models a calibrated fan that is linear in heat carried. Both the mechanism and the number were wrong.

The trade flips as the extractor improves, and only then: the optimum moves to 60 CFM at the v91 target with a 90 % laser, and to 30 CFM with the logic run hot, worth 2.0 and 7.7 W. So “localized cooling lowers fan power” is a forward claim contingent on extractor efficiency, not a present‑tense one.

What is unambiguous from the same runs is the other half: the unassisted die has no steady state anywhere from 20 to 120 CFM, and the array holds target at every one. More airflow is not the alternative — 120 CFM costs 96 W of fan and is worse on every metric. That is the argument for cooling the hotspot instead of the die.

The threshold

The chiller stops being necessary at s = 0.10

A 250 W die on 88 CFM of air sits at 381 K — over a 100 °C junction limit. Today that is answered by chilling the inlet below ambient, which costs power and puts every surface in the box below the dew point.

At a tenth of the flux, the array removes the requirement outright — the package no longer has to reach below ambient at all. This holds at every extractor efficiency in the table above, including the legacy one, because it is a feasibility threshold rather than an efficiency one. Sub‑ambient hardware does not get cheaper; it stops being needed.

That is worth more than its wattage. Condensation control is a sealing and reliability problem, not a power problem, and it is the reason sub‑ambient cooling has never gone mainstream. The array creates no sub‑ambient surface anywhere.

The reversal

Above 408 K the cooler starts exporting power

Heat lifted at T_h carries exergy φ = 1 − T₀/T_h. Near ambient that is 0.05–0.20 and the loop is mostly recycling its own pump light — which is why every honest analysis of a warm die concludes the recovery term cannot pay. That is a fact about the operating point, not about the technology.

The consequence

What a chip designed around this actually looks like

If cooling is spatially targetable, thermal uniformity stops being a virtue. The die separates by what each region’s power is made of — and the two halves want opposite temperatures.

  • Logic runs hot, deliberately. Dynamic power dominates, so temperature costs little and buys much: φ rises, recovery rises with it, and the frequency ceiling lifts. This is the region that pays for the loop.
  • Cache and Vth‑limited cells run cold. Leakage falls exponentially — roughly a halving per 10 K — and a colder device tolerates a lower threshold voltage, which raises drive current and buys frequency again without paying the cubic Vdd penalty. Two wins from one lever.
  • And that partition already exists. Static power is 34.6 % of die power on our measured floorplans and 89 % of it sits in L3 alone. Nobody has to impose the cold zone; it is already where the leakage lives.
  • The extractor changes with the zone. No single material spans 150–600 K: Yb‑doped hosts for cold storage tiles, fluoride glasses warm, Ho³⁺/Tm³⁺ fluorides for hot compute, SiC:Er or GaN:Yb above 600 K — the same wide‑bandgap families as the devices there. Per‑tile temperature targeting requires per‑tile extractor selection.
And the array has to be aimed, not merely present

Fine tile pitch is worth 2.77× on a concentrated hotspot and nothing on a uniform die — measured, across a 2000 → 50 µm ladder. Granularity is the mechanism that converts spatial structure into cooling efficacy, and a flat die has no structure to convert.

So array value cannot be quoted per part. The same hardware is worth 2.77× more on one workload than another. It is a property of the power map — which makes the floorplan and the cooling array a co‑design problem, and that is the deepest change here.

Honest

What has to be true for this

The laser is the hard part, not the extractor. The self‑powering requirement scales as [1/(ηPηcpl) − 1]/φ, which is very steep near unity: at a fixed extractor, moving the laser wall‑plug from 0.85 to 0.90 drops the crossing temperature from 716 K to 469 K, and one 20 % front‑end optical loss roughly doubles the extractor target. Every number on this page is more sensitive to the optical path than to the material.

The extractor at temperature is unproven. The efficiencies used here are targets on a published trajectory, and nothing in this model captures how a given extractor quenches at the temperature it is being asked to work at. The zone‑matched material families exist because that problem is real.

These are upper bounds by construction. The converter is held at its second‑law ceiling times a stated internal factor, so the export figures are the best a perfect device could do. They should be quoted as bounds, and the trajectory they describe matters more than any single cell.
