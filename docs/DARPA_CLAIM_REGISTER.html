Photonic Cooling Claim Register

What the simulation work will and will not support, claim by claim, so proposal text can be written against it directly. Three of these were the project’s own recorded positions until this week.

Read first

The one-sentence claim

Photonic microrefrigeration is not a cheaper way to move heat. It is the only cooling mechanism that can be aimed at a hotspot — which makes it worth having exactly where a die is hotspot‑limited, and worth more the deeper the thermal rescue, because it is the only mechanism whose cost per watt does not diverge with depth.

Everything defensible below follows from that sentence. Everything withdrawn came from trying to claim more.

What changed this week, in one line each

Materials. Yb:YLF is superseded for compute tiles; the current platforms reach 10³–10⁴ W/mm², which is where hot‑spot demand also sits. Loop model. Two errors, in opposite directions, both corrected and test‑locked. Envelope. The simulated device was 4–40× too weak, and it was the binding constraint — so a set of negative verdicts is now pending re‑run.

Measured, and it will survive questioning

measured

Array granularity is worth 2.77× on a concentrated hotspot and nothing on a uniform die.

Measured across a 2000 → 50 µm pitch ladder: 0.977 → 1.854 K/W concentrated, 0.978 → 0.670 uniform. The consequence is the claim worth making: array value cannot be quoted per die. The same hardware is worth 2.77× more on one shape than another, so it is a property of workload and floorplan rather than of the part.

measured

The three rescue mechanisms diverge by their exponents, and only the laser’s cost per watt stays flat.

More airflow goes as P ∝ R−5 — a 900 W die needs a 35.8 kW fan. A sub‑ambient chiller collapses from system COP 20 to 0.15 across 4× overload. The array’s cost per watt is flat and its recovery rises with Th. The crossover is scale‑free: 2.64× / 2.88× / 2.85× the unaided limit for fan, cold plate and microchannel alike.

A thermal rescue pays for a quarter of itself in leakage.

Holding a 34‑core die at 90.6 °C instead of 133.8 °C drops what the chip itself draws from 124.9 W to 91.7 W — 26.6 %. A thermal result, unaffected by the recovery corrections, and the largest single term no prior ledger had counted.

measured

Aimed efficacy is flat until the target stops being the die peak, then falls off a cliff — which gives a plan‑sizing rule.

1.854 K/W flat from 1 W to 16 W — exactly the target block’s own power — then marginal efficacy collapses to 0.133 by 32 W, because another block has become the hottest thing on the die. Size the plan to level the target with the runner‑up, then re‑aim rather than spend more. Coarse tiles delay the cliff substantially but never pay for it.

The design map

How hot must logic run for the loop to close?

Read the feasibility condition backwards and it says what die temperature a given extractor needs. This is the quantitative core of the “run logic hot” argument and it should lead the technical section.

The finding that should reframe the programme

ηP — the laser wall‑plug — is now the binding term, not the extractor. The requirement is [1/(ηPηcpl) − 1] / φ, which is very steep near unity. At ηASF = 0.30, moving the laser from 0.85 to 0.90 drops the self‑powering temperature from 716 K to 469 K. One 20 % front‑end optical loss roughly doubles the extractor target. The laser and the optical path are the hard part now — a different programme from the one previously costed.

At ηASF ≥ 0.30 the loop closes inside the 400–600 K hot‑compute zone the architecture proposes, so the architecture argument now closes on its own terms rather than needing a leap.

relocated

The cold zone must be a physically separate die.

A monolithic three‑zone die fails two independent ways — coarse pitch cannot address the zones, and lateral conduction shorts them anyway. Re‑run: what landed, and what has not

Landed 30 Aug — the envelope re-run, and what it moved

The simulated device had been capped at 250 W/mm², the Yb:YLF‑era figure, against the current platforms’ 10³–10⁴. Re‑run at the refreshed ceiling with second‑law recovery active, on the 34‑core die at three densities:

The array rescues a die that has no steady state, and the unpowered array does not. Control and array‑idle both run away at every density; array‑on holds 93.9–94.0 °C against a 92 °C target, on a minimum plan bracketed by bisection.

The envelope cuts the heat that must be lifted by 1.96–2.58× — 42.1 → 18.3 W at density 1.15 for the same rescue. Priced at matched efficiencies so the two corrections can be read apart, array electrical power falls 1.38–1.82×: the envelope’s 2.30× reduction in heat, partly given back by the second law’s 1.42× rise in cost per watt.

`[!]` Two cautions. The raw electrical columns are not like‑for‑like — the recorded run used a conservative efficiency preset and the re‑run the shipped defaults — which is why the matched‑efficiency figures are the ones quoted here. And the chiller comparison now has to be re‑derived: the control arm no longer reaches a steady state at all, so there is no unaided temperature to measure a required chill depth against. Until that is redone, make no quantitative chiller‑versus‑laser claim.

Blocked

Nothing above 400 K is quotable yet

31 Aug — the overnight campaign found its own ceiling

The leakage table is calibrated 310–400 K; McPAT cannot be run hotter. Above that the model either extrapolates an Arrhenius tail or clamps, and the two disagree without bound — 36× at 400 K, 600× at 450, 5820× at 500, 184 000× at 600 extrapolated, against a flat 36.1 clamped.

Extrapolated, that is unbounded positive gain inside a feedback loop: a block that nudges past 400 K gains enough leakage to jump to 500 K, which multiplies again. The signature is unmistakable — an un‑itemised accounting slab (core_other, 15.7 % of die area, power the model could not attribute) reaching 17 381 K on iteration 1, which no physical runaway does. Clamped, the code warns instead that “runaway is suppressed and results in that range are not trustworthy”.

So the two settings bracket the answer rather than giving it. Every density above about 1.8 W/mm² on these floorplans puts some block past 400 K, which is why sweeping the array envelope over 6.7× in temperature lift and 120× in intensity changed nothing: the outcome was decided by the extrapolation before the envelope mattered.

`[!]` Withdrawn on this basis: that dt_max is or is not the limiting device parameter; that the array “gives out” at any particular density; and any “enables chips that cannot otherwise run” claim at boost densities. The rescues below 400 K stand.

The fix is already the top open item. PTM SPICE cards extend the calibration above 400 K. They were identified as highest‑value for the cold zone, where the curve clamps at 310 K and the prize is uncertain 2.3×. They are now also the gate on the entire hot‑logic and high‑density programme — the regime the “run chips hotter” thesis lives in. One campaign unblocks both ends.

31 Aug, later — that campaign has been run, and the gate is largely lifted

The simulator exists. ngspice 47 built with OSDI, loading BSIM-CMG compiled from Verilog-A by OpenVAF, evaluates the ASAP7 7 nm card directly. PTM itself is gone — ptm.asu.edu no longer resolves — so ASAP7, the same group’s published successor work, stands in. It is a device and that is checked: 0.232 nA/µm at 300 K, subthreshold swing 61.7 mV/dec against a 59.5 ideal, three BSIM-CMG versions agreeing to 0.37 %.

The unbounded-extrapolation problem is gone at the hot end. The curve is now simulated across 200–500 K rather than calibrated over 310–400 K, so a block at 450 K is interpolated within data instead of extrapolated past it. The bracket that ran 36×–5820× at 400–500 K collapses to a single simulated 10.9× at 400 K and 123× at 500 K. The pipeline tail was ~47× too steep at 500 K.

`[!]` But the density ceiling moved DOWN, not up, and the prediction in this register was backwards. A gentler tail was expected to run away later. Measured, the uniform arm now fails one rung lower (1.00 W/mm² where it held at 65.99 °C). Runaway is a local instability decided by the curve’s slope at 317–347 K, not at 500 K — and there the pipeline curve is the flatter of the two, by up to 9.2×, because it clamps. So the recorded density ceilings are optimistic rather than conservative.

What this does and does not unblock. Quantities above 400 K now rest on simulated device physics rather than an unbounded extrapolation, so the reason for the blanket withdrawal is resolved. The withdrawn claims are not thereby reinstated: they were computed on the old curve and must be re-run on the new one before being quoted, and the one re-run done so far moved its answer. `[+]` The bracket that was flagged here as “the binding one” has since been run, and it binds nothing. The ceiling is decided at 310–330 K, which is where the GIDL-on and GIDL-off variants of the card disagree most in leakage level — but runaway is set by the slope, and across that band the two brackets' slopes differ by only 1.16–1.27× against the 6–14× the curve swap itself carries. Measured: the flat-die arm holds 0.80 and fails 1.00 W/mm² under both brackets, identically. The predictive-PDK GIDL uncertainty therefore does not reach the density ceiling.

`[+]` And the cold end paid off as promised. The prize the register called uncertain by 2.3× is settled: leakage falls 16.6× cooling cache 350 → 200 K where the old curve said 1.73× at every temperature because it clamps — a 2.2× larger prize, and it saturates by 280 K, so the target is barely sub-ambient rather than cryogenic. Quote the 2.2×, which is what survives every accounting choice.

`[!]` And the absolute prize is smaller than this register said. The two conversion ratios were inherited and unreproduced; they are now reproduced exactly and they are a scope error — one core’s blocks against the whole chip’s L3, on a warm‑up slice. Re‑derived on the die power the pipeline actually solves, the prize is ~6 % of die power — not the 10–30 % previously quoted, and not the book’s 31.7 %. The 2.2× improvement is untouched, because every conversion multiplies the same saturating factor by a constant, which is why it was the number to lead with.

The cold‑zone prize. Settled — and it came out smaller than either bound.

This was the register’s largest open uncertainty: 13.5 % of die power against 31.7 %, waiting on SPICE cards. The cards were built (§P0.13) and the curve re‑run.
