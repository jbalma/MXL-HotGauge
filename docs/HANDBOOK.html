Status — 20 August

Every accelerator thermal number is currently withdrawn

Read this before quoting anything below

Two defects found on 20 August invalidate the accelerator thermal results. The geometry results — plateau widths, degeneracy, placement sensitivity — follow from the power map and survive. Everything that depends on a temperature does not.

1. The leakage feedback was inert on the accelerator fixed, re-run

run_leakage_feedback built its own McPAT→floorplan name map unconditionally. That map returns None for any name it does not recognise, so on a die-shot-derived floorplan no block ever matched and the leakage update was applied to nothing. All 43 accelerator studies were constant-power solves in a project whose entire subject is temperature-dependent leakage.

They looked healthy: converged, reported residuals, passed damping verification. Three symptoms were visible and each was explained away — a residual of 0.0000 K at the first iteration, and results identical across a 60% change in leakage fraction and across two different leakage models. The second of those was reported here as a robustness result, which is the exact opposite of what it was.

Fixed with an explicit name_map parameter, plus a guard that now raises when a leakage reference is supplied and not one unit resolves. The CPU path is untouched and no CPU result moves.

2. The heatsink does not scale with die area open — blocking

BaffledFinSink returns 0.0764 K/W at 101 mm² and 0.0764 K/W at 826 mm² — identical. There is no area term. The accelerator has been cooled by a heatsink sized for a CPU eight times smaller: 400 W × 0.0764 = 30.6 K of rise before leakage even starts.

With the leakage feedback now working, the consequence is stark:

Accelerator pointas published (feedback inert) with feedback coupled
400 W uniform, 88 CFMpeak 88.06 °C, plateau 332 runaway — no steady state
700 W uniform, 88 CFMpeak 127.85 °C, plateau 170 runaway
700 W, 8/128 SMs activepeak 110.61 °C, plateau 9 runaway
…the same point with MR13 blocks, 22.3 W electrical rescued, but by cooling all 367 blocks for 687 W on a 369 W die

A 400 W part running away at 0.484 W/mm² — less than half the CPU’s measured stability cliff — is not a physical result. It is what happens when an 826 mm² die is given a CPU’s cooling. So both versions of the accelerator story are withdrawn: the first was uncoupled, the second is cooled wrongly.

The consequence for the headline result is direct. The 34× margin by which MR beat “more airflow” was measured against an unreasonably weak opponent — 88 CFM of air on a part that would ship on a cold plate. Pricing MR against a properly sized cooling solution is expected to reduce that margin substantially, and it is the comparison that actually decides deployment.

The fix in progress is a cooling specification that derives thermal resistance from real heatsink or cold-plate geometry scaled to the die, and returns the wall-plug power that geometry implies as a function of flow rate and inlet temperature — for air and liquid. See §10.

01 — The question

Can you cool a processor with light, and is it worth doing?

Maxwell Labs is developing photonic microrefrigeration (MR): a solid-state cooler that removes heat from a chip using anti‑Stokes fluorescence. A laser pumps a crystal bonded to the die; the crystal re-emits light at a shorter wavelength than it absorbed, and the energy difference comes out of the lattice as heat. The re-emitted light is carried away, and some of it is recovered as electricity by a photovoltaic cell (the “LPC”). The result is a cooler with no moving parts that can be aimed at a spot on the die roughly ten microns across.

That last property is the interesting one. Conventional cooling — a heatsink, a fan, a cold plate — removes heat from the whole package. MR can remove it from one block. The premise of the project is that this ought to be worth a great deal, because a processor’s limits are usually set by its hottest structure rather than its average temperature.

This work exists to test that premise quantitatively, and to say where it holds. The concrete deliverable is not a yes or no; it is a set of numbers a device team can aim at and a system architect can plan around.

What we are actually simulating

A processor die under a real workload, solved for its steady-state temperature field with temperature-dependent leakage in the loop — hotter silicon leaks more current, which makes it hotter still. We then add microrefrigeration to selected blocks and re-solve, and compare against the alternative of simply buying more conventional cooling.

02 — The physics, in one page

Four mechanisms explain nearly every result here

1. Leakage–temperature feedback, and thermal runaway

Transistor leakage rises steeply with temperature. Power heats the die, the die leaks more, the extra leakage is more power. At low density this converges to a steady state. Above some density it does not: the loop diverges and the part has no steady state at all. That threshold — the stability cliff — is at 1.05–1.10 W/mm² on our CPU die with 88 CFM of air.

This matters because runaway is a local phenomenon driven by the hottest blocks, and MR is a local tool. Arresting a runaway turns out to be extraordinarily cheap.

2. Constriction resistance

Heat leaving a small hot block has to spread through silicon before it reaches the heatsink. That spreading resistance is a property of the die, not of the cooler, so no amount of airflow removes it. We measure it as β − α ≈ 1.04 K/W, essentially flat from 88 to 600 CFM. It is why spinning the fan harder eventually stops helping a hotspot while continuing to cost power.

3. Thermal degeneracy — the plateau

If many blocks sit at nearly the same temperature, cooling the hottest one achieves almost nothing, because the second-hottest immediately becomes the peak. We call the set of blocks within the device’s reach of the peak the plateau, and its width is the single strongest predictor of whether hotspot cooling can help. A balanced CPU die has 15 blocks in its plateau. A uniformly loaded accelerator has 332 of 367.

4. Depth versus breadth

A cooler is limited in two independent ways: how many blocks it can cover, and how far down it can pull any one of them. These are different constraints with different fixes, and confusing them wasted a week of this project. Breadth is the plateau; depth is dt_max.

03 — Glossary

Terms, defined precisely

Several of these look like they should be related and are not. The distinctions matter enough that mixing them up has produced wrong conclusions in this project more than once.

clip‑one gain

How many kelvin the die’s peak temperature falls if you apply the cooler to exactly one block — the hottest one — and pull it down as far as the device allows.

It is a measure of degeneracy, not of the cooler. If the second-hottest block is 0.04 K below the peak, then removing the peak block entirely lowers the die by 0.04 K and no further, however good the cooler is. So clip-one gain answers: is there a hotspot here worth aiming at?

We report it against the device’s own capability. “0.040 K, 0.4% of the device” means the cooler could have delivered 10 K of lift and the die’s structure only let 0.04 K of it turn into a lower peak.

A caution learned the hard way: clip-one gain is the right metric when the plateau is wide and you cannot afford to cool all of it. When the plateau collapses to a handful of blocks, the relevant question becomes the cost of cooling the whole plateau, and clip-one understates MR badly. We drew a wrong conclusion from it once.

dt_max — “a 10 K device”

The maximum temperature lift the refrigerator can sustain: how many kelvin colder than its surroundings it can hold the spot it is cooling. Our baseline device is 10 K. This is a thermodynamic property of the cooler.

It is not the COP, and in our model the two are entirely independent. A block sitting 12.6 K above where you need it cannot be rescued by a 10 K device at any efficiency, however cheap the watts are. That is a hard wall, and it is the wall we are currently against.

h_max

Maximum cooling power density the stage can deliver, 10 W/mm². How much heat per unit area, as distinct from how deep a temperature drop. In practice this has never been the binding constraint in our runs — the plans we compute use a few percent of it.

COP, and its three flavours

Coefficient of performance — watts of heat removed per watt spent. We track three, and conflating them overstates the cooler:

  • ηASF = 0.20 — optical COP: heat removed per watt of light delivered to the crystal.
  • Electrical COP = 0.14 — ηASF × ηlaser (0.70 wall-plug). Heat removed per watt drawn from the wall.
  • Effective COP = 0.574 — after the LPC recovers 76% of the draw as electricity. This is the figure that belongs in a system power budget.

There is also a device break-even: if the recovery ratio exceeds 1, the loop returns more power than it draws. At our laser and LPC efficiencies that needs ηASF ≥ 0.587 — so the extractor, not the LPC, is the binding term there.

plateau

The number of blocks within dt_max of the peak temperature. Equivalently: how many blocks you would have to cool to realise the device’s full lift. Wide plateau ⇒ hotspot cooling is structurally the wrong tool. Narrow plateau ⇒ it may be exactly the right one.

rescue vs. holding a temperature

Two different questions that our tooling can both answer, and that we mislabelled for weeks:

Rescue — the cheapest plan that gives a runaway die any steady state. It lands wherever the stable branch happens to sit. On the CPU die at 1.10 W/mm² this costs 0.29 W and lands at 99.95 °C.

Holding a target — the cheapest plan that pins the peak to a stated temperature. Same die, same nominal 98 °C target: 131 W, because it requires cooling everything between 98 and 100 °C, which on that die is the entire plateau.

450× apart. Always quote which one you mean, and the achieved peak alongside the watts.

damping verification

Every result is re-solved at half the numerical damping; if the two disagree, the point is marked unquotable. This is not ceremony — it retired four published figures in a single day. A converged-looking number from an unconverged solve is the characteristic failure mode of this kind of simulation.

system matrix, and why it governs runtime

The thermal solver factorises a sparse matrix built from the stack geometry and the floorplan — not from the power values. So sweeping power, workload, MR target or dt_max reuses one factorisation and is nearly free; sweeping airflow, grid, or die geometry costs a full factorisation each. At accelerator scale that is 431 seconds apiece, and it is the whole of our compute budget.

That last row is a structural result rather than a detail. An 826 mm² die spreads heat laterally far better than a 101 mm² one, and dilutes the leakage feedback across eight times the area, so the local positive feedback that destabilises the CPU never closes. The accelerator does not run away where the CPU does — it simply gets too hot. Those need different interventions.

How the accelerator floorplan was built

From annotated die photographs (SemiAnalysis / Locuza): die area, TPC and L2 tile areas, PCIe PHY area, and the physical arrangement are all read off the shots. One number came from a second die (GA102): the SRAM fraction inside a compute tile, 33.4%. Without it the finest structure on the die would be a whole 3.4 mm² SM, and no hotspot smaller than that could exist by construction.

The die outline is fixed by measured area and aspect; the tile height by the measured 16-tall arrangement; the tile width by the tile’s own measured area. The interior width that results is therefore a prediction of the arrangement, not a fit — and it closes on the measured die area.

The assumption most likely to change a conclusion

Our model lets you raise dt_max from 10 K to 13 K without paying anything in efficiency. Real refrigerators do not work that way — COP falls as the temperature lift rises.

We can bound how much that matters, because the efficiency margin is enormous: at the operating point below, MR beats its alternative by 33.7×. So COP could degrade by up to 33.7× between 10 and 13 K before the conclusion flips — a 30% increase in lift causing a 3,000% collapse in efficiency, which no plausible device does. The recommendation survives, but the coupling should be modelled before anyone commits silicon to it.

What questions the pipeline can actually answer

Any of the variables below can be made the output rather than the input. The two directions asked about most often — constrain performance and solve for efficiency, or constrain efficiency and solve for performance — are both supported, though one of them only for a temperature target rather than a throughput target.

The one genuine gap is the last row. cop_breakeven.py solves for efficiency against a temperature constraint; converting a throughput target into the temperature that delivers it requires a separate clock search, and nothing chains the two. It is a modest piece of work and it is the most useful next addition, because throughput is the constraint a customer actually states.

The hybrid_cooling_optimizer is the other opportunity: it already implements both directions of the budget/performance trade and has never been run. Its premise is sound — the fan’s marginal value collapses about 90× across its range while MR’s cost stays roughly linear, and diminishing returns against linear cost is exactly the shape that produces an interior optimum rather than an all-fan or all-MR answer.

07 — Findings

Where microrefrigeration pays, and where it does not

Ordered by how confident we are, most confident first.

MR arrests thermal runaway extremely cheaply verified

A 34-core die at 1.10 W/mm² has no steady state — left alone it runs away. 0.29 W of net electrical power, applied to four blocks, gives it one, landing just under its 100 °C limit. That is 290 W of die stabilised per watt spent. Runaway is driven by a handful of blocks, and a local tool is exactly right for a local instability.

MR is weak as a clock enabler verified

On the same die, raising the sustainable clock is a different matter: +1.1% for about a watt, +2.2% for 43 W. The clock ceiling is set by the die-average thermal path, which hotspot clipping barely touches. A 50× better heatsink buys +55% clock and still never reaches the part’s rated frequency.

Cost is dominated by how much margin you demand verified

Three margin curves. The cost of a rescue rises 30–160× as the demanded target moves a few kelvin, and the knee moves with density — ~94 °C at 1.10 W/mm², ~97 °C at 1.15. It is not an absolute temperature; it tracks the top of the block distribution. Below the knee the target dips into the plateau and cooling anything means cooling everything.

The third curve is the uncomfortable one: on a liquid-class sink at the same density, the die holds 93.6 °C unaided and MR engages zero blocks. Better conventional cooling did not make MR more valuable — it made it irrelevant.

A uniformly loaded accelerator is the wrong target geometry stands; temperatures withdrawn

332 of 367 blocks within the device’s reach of the peak; clipping one buys 0.4% of its capability. All 128 compute tiles span 8.3 K — the array is effectively one thermal object. Unchanged across every leakage assumption we varied.

A realistic kernel overturns that geometry stands; temperatures withdrawn

Uniform activity was a deliberate control — so any hotspot came from geometry rather than from an imbalance assumed into the input — and it is the least favourable input MR could be given. Under a low-occupancy kernel (8 of 128 SMs active, contiguous) on a 700 W part, the plateau collapses 332 → 9 blocks on a die 10 K over its limit.

This also revealed that clip-one gain was the wrong metric here: it stays small because the active tiles are mutually degenerate, while the quantity that matters — how many blocks a rescue must cover — had fallen by 37×.

The binding constraint is lift, not cost withdrawn — see status

Five accelerator runs exited “iteration limit reached”. Quadrupling the budget moved them 1%. They had not run out of iterations — each had raised its peak by exactly dt_max and stopped. Sweeping the parameter confirmed it against a prediction registered beforehand: the achieved peak tracks uncooled − dt_max precisely, then halts on reaching the target.

So the three candidate constraints separate, and only one binds:

  • Breadth (the plateau) — collapses 332 → 9 under a concentrated kernel. Not binding.
  • Cost (the COP) — oversupplied 34×. Not binding.
  • Depth (dt_max) — needs 13 K, has 10. Binding.

What this reframes

Every earlier “MR costs too much” conclusion in this project was measured against a thermal target MR could not reach — not against a cost it could not justify. Efficiency is not the problem. Lift is. The device ask is one number: get dt_max from 10 K to 13 K.

Two caveats that bound this result

It compares MR against fans. Pump and chiller power are not modelled, and a 700 W part would not be air-cooled in practice. The comparison that decides deployment — MR against a cold plate — has not been run and cannot be, as the pipeline stands.

It is one operating point. The uniformly loaded 700 W case needs dt_max ≥ 30 K and 299 W, 43% of the die. No efficiency rescues that. MR’s case on an accelerator rests specifically on low-occupancy kernels at high TDP.

08 — Workload dependence

The answer changes with what the chip is running

This is not a caveat on the results; it is one of the results. The same die, same power, same cooler gives opposite verdicts depending on the workload’s shape.

What matters is not how much power the workload draws but how concentrated it is, and concentration has two independent axes we can vary:

  • Occupancy — how many compute tiles are active. Real GPU kernels routinely use a fraction of the machine: small grids, tail effects, partitioned inference.
  • Placement — whether the active tiles are physically adjacent. Eight neighbouring tiles pile their heat together; eight scattered across the die each sit in their own cool surroundings. Same occupancy, materially different thermal outcome.

Three kernel shapes are implemented, all real GPU behaviour rather than contrivances: occupancy (N tiles active and boosted, the rest idling), memory-bound (compute stalled, memory interface at full rate), and tensor (power shifted from each tile’s SRAM into its datapath at constant die power).

Only the first produces a hotspot. Memory-bound lands the die at 65 °C with a 171-block plateau — the interface blocks are hot together, not one of them alone. Tensor moves the plateau only 332 → 320: intra-tile concentration barely registers.

The trade that bounds every occupancy result

MR’s leverage peaks where the die is coldest. Idling tiles is what creates the concentration, and it is also what takes the power out. At 2 active tiles the clip-one gain is best in the entire study — 22% of device capability — and the die sits at 72 °C, thirty kelvin below its limit, needing no cooling at all.

Only raising total power gets you both concentration and heat. That is why the accelerator case rests on high-TDP parts running low-occupancy kernels, and not on either condition alone.

One deliberate choice cuts against finding hotspots: within a block class, power is shared equally. Any hotspot therefore comes from geometry rather than from an imbalance we assumed into the input. Real kernels are less even than this, so the results are conservative — and a genuinely skewed activity map is the most promising unexplored route to a stronger accelerator case.

09 — Open gaps

What we cannot yet answer

Ordered by how much each one could move the project’s conclusions.

  1. MR has never been priced against liquid cooling. The headline 34× is against fans. A 700 W accelerator ships on a cold plate, and our liquid points model a thermal resistance with no pump or chiller power at all — so they cannot be used as the comparison arm. If MR only beats a cooling solution nobody would fit to this part, the margin is measuring the wrong race. This is the single most important gap.
  2. Lift and efficiency are modelled as independent. Real refrigerators lose COP as ΔT rises. Our recommendation is to raise dt_max by 30% and it assumes that costs nothing. The 33.7× margin makes the conclusion robust to any plausible penalty, but the coupling is unmodelled and should not stay that way.
  3. No per-block power breakdown for the accelerator. Die shots give areas, not dissipation. Which block is hottest — and therefore what MR should aim at — rests on an assumed split. The plateau result does not, but the targeting does.
  4. No product V/F curve. The shipped table is not a modern-node curve, and the IRDS replacement is a technology metric that does not survive extrapolation to GPU clocks (it puts a 1.4 GHz part at 0.26 V against a 0.70 V nominal). Comparative results are unaffected — the curve cancels — but no clock ceiling should be quoted from either.
  5. Throughput cannot be constrained directly. We can solve for the COP that holds a temperature, but not one that delivers a throughput, which is what a customer actually specifies.
  6. The fan/MR split has never been optimised. The tool exists and has never been run. Every result to date spends on one lever or the other, and the physics says the optimum is interior.
  7. No transient results. Everything is steady-state. Pulsed pre-cooling — aiming the laser at a unit just before it fires — needs a workload trace longer and burstier than the 3.2 ms we have, in which the hot units are essentially flat.
  8. Deep memory stacks hit a solver ceiling at roughly 1.7 M unknowns, not a physical one. See below.

10 — Toolchain

What has to be built

Required to close the gaps above

  • A cooling specification — in progress, and blocking everything else. One object taking fluid, die footprint, target power, fin or channel geometry, flow rate and inlet temperature, returning the thermal resistance that geometry implies and the wall-plug power it costs. Air and liquid alike, and invertible — so a sweep can ask “what flow and inlet temperature meet this target, and what does that cost?”
  • Within it, a pump-and-chiller power model. Without one, MR cannot be compared against the cooling a high-TDP part actually ships with, and the project’s central commercial claim stays unproven.
  • A COP–ΔT coupling in the device model. Currently MRParams takes lift and efficiency as unrelated numbers.
  • Chain the clock search into the break-even solve, so a throughput target can be converted into the temperature it implies and then into a required COP.
  • Run the hybrid optimiser, and sweep efficiency across it to produce the full (budget, split, throughput, COP) surface rather than points on it.

Solver capacity

Two distinct walls, often conflated:

Speed. Factorisation dominates — 98.9% of a large accelerator run. It scales superlinearly, and the model predicted a 5.64 M-unknown factorisation at 2916 s against 2849 s measured, a 2.3% error over an 8× extrapolation. A GPU direct solver would give an estimated 3.5× on essentially all of it.

Robustness. Deep stacks fail inside SuperLU 4.3’s memory-expansion heuristic at ~1.7 M unknowns — with 629 GB of RAM free and about 2 GB of estimated fill-in. That is a 2011 solver defect, not a capacity limit, and a modern CPU solver fixes it for a fraction of the effort of a CUDA port.

The GPU trigger, as a number

A faster solver only pays in proportion to the number of distinct system matrices a study needs — and workload sweeps need one, while cooling and geometry sweeps need one per point. Break-even is at roughly 140 distinct matrices at accelerator scale, or about three to four full break-even studies.

Go at four or more matrix-diverse studies of that size. No-go while the work is workload sweeps. Two preconditions: group points by matrix first — that alone was worth 10× and cost nothing — and expose the GPUs, which currently report as absent on a node named for six of them.

A note on how this project fails

Worth recording, because it has been consistent. Nearly every apparent physical obstacle here has turned out to be an artefact: a cooling plan sized from a diverging trajectory, a convergence test that got weaker as damping tightened, a session cache constructed inside a loop, a hard-coded timeout read as a hang, an iteration limit read as a device limit, a calibration file path that silently fell back to an assumed model. Each looked like physics and was not.

The practices that catch these — damping verification on every solve, harvesting results from run output rather than transcribing them, recording a prediction before the run, and checking process liveness rather than the absence of a marker — are load-bearing, not bureaucratic. Every published figure in this project that later needed retracting was caught by one of them.

All results are damping-verified coupled 3D-ICE solves with temperature-dependent leakage, harvested from run output rather than transcribed. 311 tests passing. Device and DRAM parameters, the accelerator power split, and the V/F curve are documented assumptions, not datasheet extractions; accelerator block areas are measured from published die analysis. Nothing here is a product prediction.

Accelerator sections are marked for what survives. Their geometry — plateau widths, degeneracy, placement sensitivity — follows from the power map and stands. Every accelerator temperature is withdrawn pending a cooling model that scales with die area; see the status section. CPU results are unaffected throughout.
